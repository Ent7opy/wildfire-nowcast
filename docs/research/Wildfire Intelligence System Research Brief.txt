Operational Wildfire Intelligence: A Multi-Scale Machine Learning and MLOps Framework for Real-Time Fire Spread and Risk Management
The transition of wildfire management from reactive suppression to proactive, intelligence-driven operations is necessitated by a global shift in fire regimes. While aggregate global burned area (BA) has declined by nearly one-quarter since the early 2000s—largely due to landscape fragmentation in African savannahs—high-intensity forest fires in extratropical regions such as the Western United States, Canada, and Siberia have seen a 40% increase in extent.1 The 2024–2025 fire season underscored this urgency, with fire-related carbon emissions totaling 2.2 Pg C, roughly 9% above the 2003–2023 average and the sixth highest on record, driven by extreme seasons in South America and the Canadian boreal forests.2 Such high-impact events, including the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires that caused an estimated $250 billion in economic losses, demand a decision-grade intelligence system capable of real-time nowcasting, probabilistic spread forecasting, and dynamic risk prioritization.3
The design of a production-ready wildfire intelligence system must reconcile the "research novelty" of emerging deep learning architectures with the rigorous "operational deployability" required for life-safety decisions. This involves moving beyond static fire indices toward integrated sensing and predictive pipelines that maintain reliability even under conditions of partial data loss or sensor failure. The following sections provide a technical blueprint for such a system, focusing on the five core research pillars: fuel state estimation, event association, probabilistic verification, uncertainty policy, and risk formulation.
Executive Summary
Operational wildfire intelligence requires a multi-layered approach that integrates high-frequency geostationary observations with high-resolution polar-orbiting active fire (AF) detections. Current evidence supports the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) at 375 m resolution as the primary sensor for next-day spread prediction, consistently outperforming legacy MODIS products in spatial consistency and perimeter fidelity.4 For real-time operations, this report recommends the implementation of the Fire Event Data Suite (FEDS) for 12-hourly object-based tracking and the GOES-Observed Fire Event Representation (GOFER) for sub-hourly front tracking.5
To achieve 24–72 hour spread forecasting, the framework prioritizes Transformer-based architectures like SwinUnet, which leverage shifted-window attention to capture multi-scale spatial dependencies.7 Reliability is managed through the application of Conformal Prediction (specifically the CUQDyn algorithms), which provides non-asymptotic coverage guarantees for predictive regions, ensuring that operational decision-makers have a mathematically grounded understanding of uncertainty.8 The system’s risk surfaces are constructed using a semi-quantitative Pareto ranking, integrating hazard, exposure, and social-ecological vulnerability into a unified prioritization metric.9

A critical distinction for operational success is the selection of sensor inputs. Research indicates that while MODIS (Terra/Aqua) provides a deep historical archive, its 1 km spatial resolution is insufficient for capturing the fine-grained perimeters of rapidly expanding forest fires.10 VIIRS, currently deployed on the Suomi-NPP, NOAA-20, and NOAA-21 platforms, offers 375 m resolution, improved nighttime performance, and better sensitivity to small hotspots.11 Operational systems should utilize the NOAA-21 orbit, which flies approximately 50 minutes ahead of NOAA-20, to maximize temporal revisit density during peak afternoon burn periods.10
Fuel Moisture and Fuel-State Estimation for Operations
The physics of wildfire spread is fundamentally governed by the moisture state of the fuel bed, which determines ignition thresholds, reaction intensity, and rate of spread (ROS). Operational systems must distinguish between Dead Fuel Moisture Content (DFMC), which responds purely to atmospheric vapor exchange, and Live Fuel Moisture Content (LFMC), which is driven by plant physiological processes and soil moisture availability.
Dead Fuel Moisture Content (DFMC) Modeling and Assimilation
DFMC is categorized by time-lag classes (1-hr, 10-hr, 100-hr, 1000-hr), representing the time required for a fuel particle to achieve 63% of its equilibrium moisture content (EMC).19 For operational fire behavior prediction, 1-hour and 10-hour fuels are the most critical, as they dictate the fire's ability to cross barriers and sustain a front.
The traditional Nelson and Fosberg models remain the foundation for DFMC estimation. However, these models often fail in complex terrain due to sparse observation networks from Remote Automatic Weather Stations (RAWS). The SJSU Wildfire Interdisciplinary Research Center has addressed this through the Dead Fuel Moisture Data Assimilation (FMDA) system.19 This system uses a Trend Surface Model (TSM) to interpolate observations across topographical gradients, which are then blended with time-lag model predictions using an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). Mathematical validation of this approach shows a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.03 g/g for 10-hour fuels, significantly outperforming legacy interpolation methods.20
A persistent challenge in DFMC operations is sub-canopy estimation. Standard meteorological models often overlook the microclimatic cooling and shading effects of forest canopies. Recent research (Lyell et al., 2024) introduced a seven-day forecasting system that integrates automated fuelstick sensors with LightGBM machine learning models to predict minimum daily DFMC with an    of 0.88 for the first day of forecast.24 This "canopy climate transfer" model is critical for operational planning in densely forested regions where ROS is highly sensitive to sub-canopy wind speed and moisture.25
Live Fuel Moisture Content (LFMC) and Global Mapping
LFMC serves as a non-linear driver of wildfire intensity. Once LFMC drops below the "critical threshold"—typically around 100% for many woody species—the fuel becomes significantly more flammable, potentially leading to explosive fire growth.26
The ECMWF’s ecLand system (2025) represents the current operational peak for LFMC monitoring. By assimilating L-band Vegetation Optical Depth (VOD), Solar-Induced Fluorescence (SIF), and ASCAT backscatter into a Simplified Extended Kalman Filter, the system constrains Leaf Area Index (LAI) and soil moisture inputs.21 This multi-sensor approach allows for the generation of a daily 9 km global LFMC reanalysis (LFMC-SE) that can detect plant stress weeks before visual curing becomes apparent.27 For real-world operations, this data provides the baseline for regional risk indices and informs seasonal fire potential assessments.21
Event and Front Association from Satellite Detections
A core task of a "wildfire intelligence" system is the automated association of thousands of discrete active fire (AF) detections into coherent "fire events" with clearly defined perimeters and progression vectors.
The Fire Event Data Suite (FEDS) Algorithm
The FEDS algorithm, as implemented in NASA's FIRMS and the Suomi-NPP VIIRS products, uses an alpha-shape approach to estimate the perimeter of ongoing fire events every 12 hours.5 FEDS is preferred for operational use because it:
1. Clusters discrete detections based on spatial and temporal proximity.
2. Delineates the active fire line from the burned area, providing behavior metrics for each 12-hour growth increment.
3. Tracks fire growth for large events (>5 km²) in sequence, allowing for the reconstruction of historical fire runs.5
While FEDS provides a robust 12-hourly "snapshot," it cannot capture the sub-hourly dynamics of wind-driven spread. To address this, the GOES-Observed Fire Event Representation (GOFER) algorithm leverages geostationary observations (GOES-East/West) to derive hourly progression.6 GOFER uses a dynamic smoothing kernel and parallax terrain correction to rectify the displacement caused by the high viewing angle of geostationary sensors over complex terrain.28
Filtering Industrial Heat Sources: The FlareSat Benchmark
Operational reliability is often compromised by "false alarms" from non-wildfire heat sources, predominantly gas flares. The Global Gas Flaring Tracker (July 2025) noted that flaring volumes rose to 151 billion cubic meters in 2024, creating persistent thermal anomalies in oil-producing regions like the Permian Basin and the Middle East.29
To prevent these from contaminating fire spread models, the system must implement a semantic segmentation filter based on the FlareSat benchmark.16 FlareSat provides 7,337 labeled patches from Landsat 8, including onshore and offshore flares alongside challenging false positives like active wildfires and urban reflective surfaces.17 Deep learning models (e.g., U-Net with attention layers) trained on FlareSat can distinguish flares from wildfires by leveraging the extreme temperatures (>1,100°C) and spatial persistence of industrial stacks compared to the dynamic movement of wildfire fronts.17
Probabilistic Verification and Model Promotion for Rare Spatial Events
Wildfire spread is a "spatially rare event," occurring in only a small fraction of grid cells. Traditional pixel-wise verification scores, such as the Brier score or Accuracy, are insufficient because they suffer from the "double-penalty" problem.14 If a model predicts a fire front that is perfectly accurate in shape and magnitude but is offset by a single pixel, it is penalized twice (one false positive and one false negative). This encourages models to be "blurry" or overly conservative to maximize pixel-wise scores.
Spatially Enhanced Loss Functions (SELFs)
To solve the double-penalty problem in training and evaluation, the system should adopt Spatially Enhanced Loss Functions (SELFs). SELFs integrate spatial verification methods directly into the neural network's loss function.14
* Neighborhood (Fuzzy) SELFs: These use a patch (e.g., 3x3 or 5x5) centered at each pixel to compare the fraction of fire detections. This allows for spatial leeway, rewarding the model for "getting the neighborhood right" even if the exact pixel is slightly displaced.14
* Scale-Separation (Spectral) SELFs: These apply Fourier or wavelet decomposition to both predictions and observations, isolating different physical scales (e.g., fine-scale spot fires vs. regional fire fronts).14
Models trained with spectral filters have been shown to produce significantly better-calibrated probabilities than standard pixel-wise models.14 For operational deployment, the Fractions Skill Score (FSS) is the recommended metric for determining the "useful scale" of a forecast—the spatial resolution at which the model's predictive power exceeds a random baseline.14
Model Promotion Criteria: The Champion-Challenger Lifecycle
In a mission-critical MLOps environment, model promotion must be gated by rigorous performance thresholds and "shadow mode" evaluation.32
1. Baseline Monitoring: The current "Champion" model's performance is monitored for data drift (changes in input distributions) and concept drift (changes in fire behavior relationships).
2. Challenger Evaluation: New models (e.g., transitioning from SwinUnet to LinU-Mamba) are run in parallel with production data but do not serve predictions to end-users.34
3. Promotion Gates: A challenger is only promoted to champion if it meets three criteria on a "golden dataset" of historical extreme events:
   * Accuracy: >3% improvement in F1 score or AUPRC.33
   * Latency: Inference must be completed in <50ms per patch to ensure real-time nowcasting capability.35
   * Calibration: The Brier Skill Score must be positive and stable across diverse ecological regions (e.g., Western Boreal vs. Mediterranean shrublands).31
Uncertainty, Abstention, and Fail-Safe Policy Design
Wildfire intelligence systems must handle uncertainty not just as a statistical output, but as an operational constraint. An overconfident spread forecast can lead to catastrophic misallocations of suppression resources or, worse, civilian fatalities.
Conformal Prediction for Operational Guarantees
While Bayesian methods for uncertainty quantification (UQ) are conceptually sound, they are often computationally expensive and struggle to scale for high-dimensional gridded wildfire models.8 Conformal Prediction offers a distribution-free alternative that provides "non-asymptotic marginal guarantees".8
The CUQDyn1 and CUQDyn2 algorithms, developed for dynamic systems, are the recommended standards for operational UQ.8 CUQDyn2, optimized for large-scale models, uses a "single global quantile" for calibration derived from standardized residuals.8 This ensures that the probability of the true fire extent falling within the predicted "prediction region" meets a target level (e.g., 90% or 95%) even if the underlying model is misspecified or the data is noisy.13
Fail-Open and Fail-Closed Policy Design
The system must operate under a clearly defined "fallback" strategy to handle data outages or high-uncertainty events.38
* Fail-Closed (Safety Critical): For automated evacuation-triggering thresholds, the system should adopt a "fail-closed" policy. If input data from VIIRS is missing and GOES confidence is low, the system ceases automated recommendations and alerts human analysts. This prevents "hallucinated" spread predictions from being served during critical decision windows.38
* Fail-Open (Availability focus): For general situational awareness (e.g., a "fire map" for the public), the system can "fail-open" by degrading to a persistence-based baseline (assuming the fire will stay where it was last detected) while clearly flagging the information as "Stale" or "Low Confidence".38
* Abstention Policy: The model should be programmed to "abstain" from making a prediction if the width of the conformal prediction region exceeds an operational utility threshold. In such cases, the uncertainty is too great to provide an actionable forecast, and the system should instead output a "Hazard Warning" rather than a specific spread map.8
Wildfire Risk Index Formulation
Risk is not merely the probability of a fire, but the intersection of that probability with the potential for loss. An operational risk surface must integrate hazard, exposure, and vulnerability (HEV) into a dynamic metric for prioritization.
The EFFIS Semi-Quantitative Methodology
The European Forest Fire Information System (EFFIS) provides a harmonized Wildfire Risk Assessment (WRA) methodology that serves as a global blueprint.9
1. Hazard (Fire Danger): Derived from the Fire Weather Index (FWI) and refined with real-time fuel moisture (LFMC/DFMC) data.
2. Vulnerability: Assessed across three categories: People (population density), Ecological values (biodiversity), and Economic values (infrastructure).9
3. Exposure: The placement and orientation of structures relative to predicted ember attack, radiant heat, and direct flame contact.40
EFFIS employs a "Pareto ranking" system for integration, which ensures stability across transformations of the underlying components (e.g., changing units or percentile scales).9 In a production environment, this allows the system to identify "high-priority areas" where multiple model instances agree on extreme risk levels, effectively identifying the "tail risk" profile associated with catastrophic fire events.41
Risk to the Built Environment
A critical gap in many regional indices is the failure to model "structure-to-structure" (S2S) ignition processes. Research from Headwaters Economics (August 2025) emphasizes that wildfire risk in the wildland-urban interface (WUI) is driven by localized characteristics like building materials, housing density, and the "home ignition zone".40 An operational system must integrate these secondary modifiers—such as mechanical thinning credits or defensible space compliance—to provide "risk-appropriate" prioritization for fire management agencies.41

Research-to-Implementation Roadmap: 2026–2028
The deployment of an operational wildfire intelligence system must bridge the gap between "notebook research" and "production systems" over a three-year iterative cycle.
Phase 1: Near-Real-Time Foundation (Months 1–6)
* Data Pipeline: Establish VPC-only ingestion for VIIRS 375 m NRT (NASA FIRMS) and HRRR/RRFS hourly weather models.32
* Filtering: Integrate the FlareSat segmentation model to automatically mask gas flares and industrial heat sources from the nowcasting layer.16
* Fuel State: Deploy the SjSU FMDA Kalman-filter system for hourly 10-hour dead fuel moisture mapping at 2 km resolution.19
Phase 2: Predictive Spread and Uncertainty (Months 7–18)
* Core Forecasting: Transition the forecasting engine to SwinUnet using multi-day inputs (5-day snapshots) for 24-hour spread prediction.7
* Safety Layer: Implement Conformal Prediction (CUQDyn2) to generate probabilistic prediction regions with a 90% non-asymptotic coverage guarantee.8
* Evaluation Framework: Automate "Champion/Challenger" shadow testing, requiring challengers to show a >3% F1 improvement on the WildfireSpreadTS (WFTS) benchmark.7
Phase 3: Risk Prioritization and Full Automation (Months 19–36)
* Risk Surfaces: Implement the Pareto-ranking EFFIS risk methodology at 100 m scale, incorporating secondary modifiers like structure-to-structure (S2S) ignition and home hardening.9
* Scale Operations: Move from manual retraining to event-driven automated pipelines that trigger upon detection of significant data or concept drift.32
* Governance: Formalize abstention and fail-safe policies, including manual overrides for safety-critical evacuation nodes.38

Decision Playbook: Deploying Wildfire Intelligence
For the System Architect and Chief Scientist, these are the non-negotiable decision points for building a production-grade system.
1. Sensor Selection: Do not use 1 km MODIS as a primary input for spread models. Mandate VIIRS 375 m (I-band) for its superior ability to resolve fire lines in complex topography.4
2. Training Logic: Never train using a pixel-wise loss function alone. Integrate Spatially Enhanced Loss Functions (SELFs) such as neighborhood-based Brier scores to ensure the model captures the correct "fire structure" rather than just pixel-perfect accuracy.14
3. Uncertainty Management: Reject Bayesian uncertainty for real-time gridded outputs. Use Conformal Prediction (CUQDyn) to provide a distribution-free "prediction set" that is guaranteed to contain the fire front 90% of the time.8
4. Policy of Abstention: In conditions of high atmospheric noise or sensor failure, the model must "abstain" from automated spread prediction. Operational fallback should revert to a "High Uncertainty Warning" served to a human analyst rather than providing a potentially incorrect binary mask.13
5. Promotion Gates: A new model version must pass through a "Shadow Deployment" where its latency is verified at <50ms and its Brier Skill Score is proven to be superior to the current Champion over a 10,000-sample calibration set.32
6. Risk Integration: Risk surfaces must be updated hourly. Hazard (Fire Potential) is dynamic, but Exposure and Vulnerability must be refreshed seasonally to account for new construction and community-scale fuel treatments.9
